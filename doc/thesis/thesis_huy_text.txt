                                            ABSTRACT

         Large language models (LLMs) have opened practical opportunities for automat-
ing parts of educational content creation. In assessment, however, generating high-quality
multiple-choice questions (MCQs) remains difficult. Lecturers still need questions that
are tied to the taught material, have a clear answer key, use plausible distractors, and
fit the expected exam style, difficulty distribution, and cognitive level. Generic LLM
outputs can be fluent, but they may also hallucinate unsupported content or ignore local
assessment patterns.

         In this thesis, I propose QuizGen, a pattern-aware retrieval-augmented gener-
ation (RAG) platform for generating grounded multiple-choice questions from lecture
materials. QuizGen addresses the limitations of generic generation by combining lecture-
material retrieval, exam-pattern conditioning, and Bloom's taxonomy control within a
unified web-based workflow.

         QuizGen works as a multi-stage pipeline. Uploaded lecture documents in PDF,
DOCX, and PPTX formats are extracted and split into overlapping word chunks with a
chunk size of 2,000 words and an overlap of 200 words. These chunks are embedded
through the configured embedding provider and stored for semantic retrieval. When a
lecturer requests a quiz, cosine similarity retrieves the most relevant chunks and injects
them into a structured prompt, optionally alongside an exam-pattern template extracted
from past exams. The prompt also encodes the target Bloom's taxonomy level and
difficulty distribution, so the LLM returns questions with answer options, the correct
answer, an explanation, a Bloom label, and a difficulty rating. Grounding evaluation is
available as an explicit review step via the evaluation endpoint, which measures semantic
overlap with the source document and flags each item as well grounded, partially

grounded, or poorly grounded. After review, the questions become a playable quiz;
attempts, scores, timings, and Bloom-level accuracy are stored and shown in dashboard

         Evaluation on ten topics in English and Vietnamese, with three repeated runs
and six questions per topic, shows that RAG improves semantic grounding over vanilla
LLM generation. Exam-pattern conditioning further improves Bloom-level distribution
alignment. Among the tested baselines, the full system achieves the highest LLM judge
quality score while returning the full question set in every run.

         Several limitations remain. The evaluation dataset is thesis-scale and may not
generalize to all domains or languages. The grounding score is a semantic proxy, not a

                                   CHAPTER I

                               INTRODUCTION

1.1 Background
         Artificial intelligence has increasingly become part of educational technology,

especially in tasks that involve reading, summarizing, tutoring support, and content
preparation. Large language models (LLMs) are particularly relevant because they can
produce fluent natural-language outputs from relatively small prompts. For assessment,
this capability is useful but also risky: many courses need frequent quizzes and exam-
style questions, yet those questions must still reflect what was actually taught.

         Multiple-choice questions (MCQs) are widely used because they are structured,
easy to grade automatically, and suitable for both formative practice and summative
examination. A well-designed MCQ can test factual recall, conceptual understanding,
and higher-order reasoning. For students, MCQs also provide a practical way to review
course knowledge and identify weaknesses before an exam.

         However, producing good MCQs is not a simple task. Each question must be
relevant to the lecture materials and have one clearly correct answer. Distractors should
be plausible without being misleading, and the overall difficulty level should match
what students have actually been taught. When a course contains many lecture slides
or reading materials, producing a large number of questions by hand becomes time-
consuming. Question quality also varies with the lecturer's available time, experience,
and familiarity with the intended assessment style.

         LLMs can generate questions quickly, but direct generation without source

control may produce questions that are fluent but unsupported by the actual teaching
materials. In an academic setting, generated questions should be traceable to the content
that students have learned. For that reason, automation alone is not enough; the genera-
tion process also needs grounding in lecture materials and alignment with the desired
exam style.

         These observations motivate the development of QuizGen, a pattern-aware
retrieval-augmented generation system for creating multiple-choice questions from
lecture materials. I designed it to help lecturers generate reviewable questions and to
give students a way to practice those questions inside a web-based learning workflow.

1.2 Motivating Scenario and Use Cases

         A typical motivating scenario is a lecturer preparing a weekly practice quiz after
finishing a lecture topic. The lecturer may already have a PDF slide deck, a DOCX
handout, or a PPTX lecture file, but still needs to turn that material into questions that
match the style of the course. Preparing all questions manually requires repeated reading,
selection of assessable concepts, distractor drafting, answer-key checking, and revision
of weak items. The work is pedagogically important, but much of it is also repetitive.

         From the student's perspective, the problem appears differently. Students often
want more practice questions than the lecturer can reasonably prepare by hand. They also
need quick feedback after answering: whether their selected answer is correct, why the
correct option is correct, and which area of the course they should review next. A static
list of generated questions is useful, but it becomes more valuable when it is connected
to a practice interface, attempt history, and progress visualization.

         QuizGen targets three concrete use cases. The first is lecturer-assisted quiz
drafting: a lecturer uploads lecture materials, optionally selects a saved exam pattern,

generates a set of MCQs, and reviews grounding signals before using the questions. The
second is student self-practice: a student opens a saved quiz, answers the questions,
and reviews explanations immediately after submission. The third is course analytics:
repeated attempts are collected so the application can show score trends and Bloom-level
weaknesses over time.

         These use cases clarify a design principle that runs through the whole thesis. The
goal is not to replace the lecturer as the final authority on assessment quality. Instead it is
to reduce the mechanical effort needed to produce draft questions, while keeping enough
traceability for the lecturer to make an informed decision. For this reason QuizGen pairs
source retrieval with a grounding evaluator and a review interface, rather than exposing
raw LLM output as a finished exam artifact.

1.3 Problem Statement

         Current MCQ creation still depends heavily on manual effort. In a typical
course, lecturers need to review lecture materials, identify important concepts, decide
the intended difficulty level, write the question stem, prepare answer options, and verify
that the final question is correct. This process is repetitive and can become burdensome
when many quizzes are needed across different topics.

         Recent LLMs offer a promising solution because they can produce natural-
language questions with little input. Nevertheless, using an LLM directly for academic
question generation introduces several problems.

         First, generated questions may not be grounded in the uploaded lecture materials.
A model may rely on its general knowledge and generate content that appears reasonable
but is not actually covered in the course. In educational assessment, this is a serious issue
because students should be tested on the intended learning materials, not on unsupported

external content.
         Second, generic LLM outputs may not follow a specific exam pattern. In practice,

each course may have its own style of questions, answer-option structure, and difficulty
distribution. Without additional conditioning, generated questions may be inconsistent
with the style that lecturers expect.

         Third, it is difficult to control the cognitive level of generated questions. Educa-
tional design often uses Bloom's taxonomy to distinguish between different levels of
learning, such as remembering, understanding, applying, and analyzing. If this distribu-
tion is not controlled, the generated quiz may contain too many simple recall questions
or may fail to match the intended learning objectives.

         Fourth, many question generation approaches stop at producing question text.
They do not provide a complete workflow for students to attempt the generated quiz,
review their answers, track scores, or observe learning progress over time. As a result,
the generated questions are less useful as part of a practical learning system.

         The main problem addressed in this thesis is how to design and implement a
system that can generate exam-style MCQs grounded in uploaded lecture materials,
aligned with a target question pattern, and usable within an interactive quiz practice
workflow.

1.4 Scope and Objectives

         The main objective is to design, implement, and evaluate QuizGen as a web-
based system for generating grounded and pattern-aware multiple-choice questions from
lecture materials.

         My focus is on a complete workflow rather than on a single algorithm. A lecturer
can upload lecture materials, configure or select a question pattern, generate MCQs,

quiz, view their results, and track performance through the application.

         The specific objectives are:

         · To build a document processing pipeline that supports lecture materials in PDF,

         · To apply retrieval-augmented generation so that generated questions are based
           on relevant content chunks retrieved from the uploaded materials.

         · To incorporate exam-pattern conditioning so that generated questions can better

         · To generate structured MCQ outputs, including question text, answer options,

         · To provide a grounding evaluation mechanism that helps identify whether a

         · To implement a web application that supports quiz generation, review, practice,
           attempt history, and performance visualization.

         · To evaluate the system using thesis-scale experiments and compare the full

production platform. The current implementation uses a SQLite database and integrates
LLM-based services for embedding, retrieval, and generation. Evaluation is conducted
on a limited dataset covering English and Vietnamese topics.

1.5 Research Questions

         The thesis is guided by three research questions that correspond to the main
technical components of QuizGen.

        1. RQ1: Does retrieval-augmented generation improve the source ground-
           ing of generated MCQs compared with direct LLM prompting? This
           question examines whether retrieving lecture chunks before generation makes
           the generated questions more semantically aligned with the uploaded source
           material.

        2. RQ2: Does exam-pattern conditioning improve alignment with the in-
           tended Bloom-level distribution? This question evaluates whether few-shot
           examples and explicit distribution instructions can steer the LLM toward a
           desired cognitive-level mix without model fine-tuning.

        3. RQ3: Can the generated questions be integrated into a usable web-based
           practice workflow? This question focuses on the practical system outcome:
           upload, generation, review, quiz attempt submission, attempt review, usage
           tracking, and dashboard analytics.

1.6 Assumptions and Proposed Solution

         The work assumes that uploaded lecture materials are relevant, readable, and
sufficiently informative for question generation. It also assumes that lecturers or autho-

intended application context is a university or classroom environment where lecturers
need practice or exam-style questions, and students need additional opportunities for
self-practice.

         To address the identified problems, I propose QuizGen, a pattern-aware retrieval-
augmented generation system. It combines semantic retrieval and exam-pattern condi-
tioning with Bloom-level control. A grounding evaluation component and a quiz practice
interface complete the workflow inside a single web application.

         The workflow begins with document upload and text extraction. Extracted lecture
content is divided into overlapping chunks, and each chunk is converted into a vector
embedding. When a quiz is generated, the backend retrieves the most relevant chunks
using semantic similarity and inserts them into the generation prompt. The prompt may
also include an exam pattern derived from sample questions, together with the desired
difficulty and Bloom-level distribution.

         Each generated MCQ carries a question stem, four answer options, the correct
answer, an explanation, a difficulty label, and a Bloom's taxonomy label. A grounding
evaluator then scores how well each question is supported by the source material. Re-
viewed questions become a playable quiz. As students attempt the quiz, their answers,
scores, timing, and per-Bloom correctness are stored and surfaced through the dashboard.

                           LITERATURE REVIEW

2.1 Large Language Models in Education
         Large language models (LLMs) are neural networks trained on massive text

corpora that can produce fluent, contextually appropriate natural language across a wide
range of tasks [1]. In education, LLMs have attracted growing interest as tools for content
summarization, intelligent tutoring, automated grading, and question generation. Xu et
al. [2] provide a comprehensive survey of LLM applications in education, organising the
landscape around four pillars: student assistance, teacher assistance, adaptive learning,
and educational assessment. The survey concludes that LLMs "perform outstandingly in
improving teaching efficiency and interactivity" but also face substantial challenges in
data privacy, model transparency, and domain-specific factual accuracy.

         A parallel survey by Xu et al. [3] extends this analysis by cataloguing benchmarks
and evaluation datasets specific to educational AI. The authors observe that most existing
systems focus on general-purpose question answering and reading comprehension rather
than structured assessment generation tied to lecture materials. This gap motivates
research that places LLM generation within a curriculum-grounded pipeline.

         Empirical studies confirm both the promise and the limitations of LLMs for
classroom use. A systematic review published in Frontiers in Education [4] examined 47
studies and found that ChatGPT-based tools improve student engagement and provide
useful formative feedback, but show uneven performance across subjects and tend to
reduce critical-thinking demands when used without appropriate scaffolding. A case

Page 22 of 103 - AI Writing Submission  Submission ID trn:oid:::1:3572025423
Page 23 of 103 - AI Writing Submission  Submission ID trn:oid:::1:3572025423

study by Pham et al. [5] at IEEE BigData 2023 evaluated ChatGPT (GPT-3.5-turbo) for
question generation from passage context and demonstrated that prompt engineering
alone, without retrieval grounding, can produce competitive outputs in well-scoped
domains.

         Hallucination remains the most critical risk for educational deployment. Danyaro
et al. [6] identify hallucination as a fundamental reliability barrier, noting that LLMs
may produce plausible but factually incorrect statements, particularly in specialised
academic domains. The authors recommend knowledge injection, human review loops,
and source-grounded generation as primary mitigation strategies. These findings directly
motivate the retrieval-augmented approach adopted in this thesis.

         The recent LLM education landscape also continues a longer history of adaptive
educational technology. Intelligent tutoring systems (ITS) were already being studied in
the 1980s as systems that combine a domain model, a student model, and pedagogical
feedback rules. Anderson, Boyle, and Reiser [13] describe computer tutors for geometry
proofs and LISP programming that were grounded in cognitive theory rather than in
general text generation. This lineage is important because modern LLM tutors do not
introduce the educational goal of adaptive support from scratch; instead, they provide a
new language-interface layer on top of a much older aim: diagnosing learner needs and
responding with timely, personalised guidance.

         Contemporary products demonstrate how this lineage is being reinterpreted with
generative models. Khan Academy's Khanmigo is presented as an AI-powered tutor
and teaching assistant that guides students rather than simply giving direct answers, and
also offers teacher-facing tools such as lesson planning, rubrics, and exit tickets [15].
Duolingo Max similarly uses GPT-4-powered features for language learning, including
interactive roleplay and answer explanation features that give learners feedback inside

Page 24 of 103 - AI Writing Submission  Submission ID trn:oid:::1:3572025423

the practice flow [16]. These systems show that LLMs are increasingly embedded inside
complete learning products, not merely used as standalone chatbots.

         Automated assessment has an even older technical history. Automated essay
scoring systems such as Project Essay Grade (PEG) were proposed in the 1960s and
later evolved into large-scale writing assessment tools [14]. The relevance to MCQ
generation is methodological: automated educational systems must be evaluated not only
for linguistic fluency but also for validity, reliability, and alignment with the intended
assessment construct. QuizGen therefore treats generation quality as a combination of
source grounding, cognitive-level alignment, answer correctness, and post-generation
review rather than as a pure text generation problem.

2.2 Retrieval-Augmented Generation

         Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) [7] is a framework that augments a
generative language model with a retrieval component. Given an input query, the
retrieval module selects relevant passages from an external knowledge store; these
passages are then concatenated with the query and passed to the generator as additional
context. Lewis et al. [7] demonstrate that this architecture outperforms closed-book
generation on knowledge-intensive NLP tasks such as open-domain question answering
and fact verification, because the retrieved context constrains the generator to produce
responses supported by authoritative source material.

         In the context of educational question generation, RAG addresses the core weak-
ness of vanilla LLM prompting: the tendency to hallucinate content not covered in
the course. By indexing lecture materials and retrieving the most relevant chunks at
generation time, the system ensures that each MCQ is anchored to a specific segment of
the uploaded document. This traceability is particularly valuable in academic settings

                                                   12

Page 24 of 103 - AI Writing Submission  Submission ID trn:oid:::1:3572025423
Page 25 of 103 - AI Writing Submission  Submission ID trn:oid:::1:3572025423

where assessment questions must be justifiable from the course syllabus.
         Evaluating RAG systems introduces its own methodological challenges. Es et

al. [8] propose RAGAS, a reference-free evaluation framework that measures retrieval
quality (context recall and precision) and generation quality (faithfulness and answer
relevance) without requiring gold-standard annotations. RAGAS treats faithfulness,
defined as the degree to which generated statements are entailed by the retrieved context,
as the primary grounding signal. This thesis adopts a semantic grounding score inspired
by this principle, adapted for the MCQ generation domain where ground-truth answer
keys are available but annotated question quality is not.

         RAG depends first on the quality of retrieval. Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR)
showed that open-domain passage retrieval can be implemented with learned dense
representations using a dual-encoder architecture, rather than relying only on sparse
lexical matching such as BM25 [17, 18]. This shift is relevant to lecture-slide retrieval
because students and lecturers may request a topic using vocabulary that differs from
the exact wording in the source document. Dense embeddings provide a way to retrieve
passages by semantic similarity, which is the basis of the cosine-similarity search used
in QuizGen.

         Sparse and dense retrieval are complementary rather than mutually exclusive.
BM25 remains useful for exact terms, abbreviations, and contrastive wording, while
dense retrieval can recover semantically related passages that do not share the same
surface vocabulary. Reciprocal Rank Fusion provides a simple way to combine multiple
ranked lists without training a new ranker [19]. Neural passage reranking, such as BERT-
based reranking, further motivates a second-stage model that scores candidate passages
after an initial retrieval step [20]. These methods inform the post-review retrieval
ablation, although QuizGen keeps the dense retriever as the default implementation for

Page 26 of 103 - AI Writing Submission  Submission ID trn:oid:::1:3572025423

the main thesis comparison.
         Subsequent RAG variants show different ways to connect retrieved evidence

to the generator. Fusion-in-Decoder (FiD) encodes retrieved passages separately and
fuses them during decoding, improving open-domain question answering when many
passages must be considered [21]. REPLUG takes a more black-box-friendly approach
by prepending retrieved documents to the input of an existing language model and
using the language model to supervise retriever improvement [22]. QuizGen follows
the simpler black-box pattern because the implemented system calls hosted LLM APIs
rather than training or modifying model internals.

         RAG also introduces failure modes that are important for educational use. The
retriever may select chunks that are semantically close but pedagogically irrelevant, may
miss a small but essential definition, or may retrieve too many loosely related chunks and
dilute the useful context. Even when the right chunk is retrieved, the generator can still
ignore part of the evidence or combine it with unsupported prior knowledge. Evaluation
frameworks such as RAGAS [8] and ARES [23] respond to this problem by separating
dimensions such as context relevance, faithfulness, and answer relevance. QuizGen uses
a smaller thesis-scale grounding proxy, but the design follows the same principle: source
support must be measured separately from general answer fluency.

2.3 Prompt Engineering and Few-Shot Learning

         Prompt engineering refers to the systematic design of input instructions to elicit
desired behaviours from a pre-trained language model without updating its parameters
[10]. It offers a lightweight alternative to fine-tuning, requiring no labelled training data
and no GPU-intensive optimisation, making it practical for domain-specific applications
where training data is scarce or expensive to collect.

                                                   14

Page 26 of 103 - AI Writing Submission  Submission ID trn:oid:::1:3572025423
Page 27 of 103 - AI Writing Submission  Submission ID trn:oid:::1:3572025423

         White et al. [9] introduce a prompt pattern catalog that formalises common
prompting strategies as reusable templates. Relevant patterns for this thesis include the
Persona pattern (assigning the model a role such as "You are a university lecturer in
Computer Science"), the Template pattern (providing output schemas such as JSON
structures for MCQ fields), and the Few-shot example pattern (including representative
questions in the prompt to demonstrate the target style). Together, these patterns form
the basis of exam-pattern conditioning in QuizGen.

         Few-shot learning [1] allows a model to generalise from a small number of
in-context examples. In the QuizGen pipeline, sample questions extracted from past
exams serve as few-shot demonstrations of the desired question style, difficulty level,
and answer-option structure. This conditioning mechanism does not require access to
model weights and is therefore compatible with API-based LLM providers. A detailed
description of the prompt structure and conditioning logic is provided in Chapter III.

         Prompting research also distinguishes between surface formatting control and
reasoning control. Chain-of-thought prompting asks a model to use intermediate rea-
soning steps before giving an answer, and Wei et al. [26] show that this can improve
performance on arithmetic, commonsense, and symbolic reasoning tasks for sufficiently
large models. Self-consistency extends this idea by sampling multiple reasoning paths
and selecting the answer that appears most consistently across those paths [27]. QuizGen
does not expose hidden reasoning traces to students, but these methods justify why
prompt structure matters for higher-order questions: application and analysis items often
require the model to connect concepts rather than merely restate definitions.

         The implemented prompt therefore uses direct constraints instead of free conver-
sation. It assigns the model an exam-generator role, supplies retrieved source material,
optionally adds lecturer-provided examples, and requires a JSON schema containing

2.4 Bloom's Taxonomy and Difficulty Control

         Bloom's taxonomy [11] organises cognitive learning objectives into six hierar-
chical levels: Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate, and Create. Originally
published in 1956 and revised in 2001, the framework remains a standard tool in educa-
tional design for specifying and assessing the cognitive demands of learning activities.

         The revised taxonomy by Anderson and Krathwohl reframes the cognitive process
dimension using verb forms­remember, understand, apply, analyze, evaluate, and create­
and separates this from the knowledge dimension [12]. This revision is better suited to
automated generation than the original noun-based hierarchy because prompts can ask
for observable question behaviours, such as "explain", "apply", or "analyze". QuizGen
adopts the revised labels in the prompt and database schema, while retaining Bloom's
taxonomy as the educational framework.

         In the context of automated question generation, Bloom's taxonomy serves two
distinct functions. First, it provides a vocabulary for controlling the cognitive level of
generated questions: by encoding a target Bloom label in the generation prompt, the
system can steer the LLM toward questions that require recall, application, or analysis.
Second, it provides a metric for evaluating the cognitive balance of a generated question
set: the Kullback­Leibler (KL) divergence between the target Bloom distribution and
the observed distribution of generated labels measures how closely the system honours
the intended difficulty mix.

         Research in neural question generation confirms the practical relevance of this

Page 29 of 103 - AI Writing Submission  Submission ID trn:oid:::1:3572025423

control mechanism. A survey of recent advances in neural question generation by Pan
et al. [24] identifies cognitive-level targeting as an open challenge, noting that most
existing systems generate lower-order recall questions by default and require explicit
conditioning to produce higher-order analytical questions. QuizGen addresses this
limitation by including the target Bloom level and difficulty distribution as explicit
prompt parameters, and by measuring KL divergence as a primary evaluation metric.

         Automatic question generation research reaches the same conclusion from a
broader educational perspective. Kurdi et al. [25] review automatic question generation
for educational purposes and show that the field spans rule-based, semantic, and neural
methods, but still requires attention to pedagogical validity and question type. For this
thesis, Bloom labels are not treated as guaranteed truth simply because the LLM outputs
them. They are used as controllable metadata that can be checked in aggregate against a
target distribution and reviewed manually for high-stakes use.

2.5 Educational Assessment Requirements for MCQ Quality

         Although automatic question generation is often evaluated with language similar-
ity or fluency metrics, educational assessment requires a broader definition of quality. A
generated MCQ is useful only if it satisfies both linguistic and pedagogical requirements.
The question stem should be clear, unambiguous, and focused on one assessable concept.
The answer options should include exactly one correct answer, and the distractors should
be plausible enough to reveal misconceptions rather than being obviously incorrect. The
explanation should connect the correct answer back to the learning material so that the
question can support formative feedback.

         For lecture-grounded generation, relevance is not sufficient. A question may
be relevant to the general subject area while still being unsupported by the uploaded

Page 29 of 103 - AI Writing Submission  Submission ID trn:oid:::1:3572025423
Page 30 of 103 - AI Writing Submission  Submission ID trn:oid:::1:3572025423

lecture slides. For example, an operating systems quiz about CPU scheduling should not
introduce a scheduling algorithm that was not covered in the specific lecture material
used for the course. This distinction is central to QuizGen: the system treats source
support as a separate dimension from natural-language quality.

         Another requirement is alignment with the intended assessment pattern. Many
courses develop recognizable styles over time, such as short definition questions,
scenario-based application questions, or numerical reasoning questions. A generic LLM
can produce grammatically correct MCQs, but the questions may feel inconsistent with
the lecturer's existing exam style. Few-shot pattern conditioning provides a practical
way to express this style requirement without collecting a large training dataset.

         Finally, MCQs used for practice should be actionable after submission. Students
benefit when the system records their attempt, shows explanations, and helps them
identify which cognitive levels remain weak. This requirement extends the scope of
question generation from a one-time text generation task into a learning application. It
is the reason this thesis includes attempt storage, confidence trend visualization, and
Bloom-level analytics as part of the implemented system.

2.7 Evaluation Approaches for Generated Questions

         Evaluating generated MCQs is more difficult than evaluating ordinary classifica-
tion output because several properties must be considered at the same time. A question
may be grammatical but unsupported by the source material, grounded but too easy, or
aligned with the intended Bloom level but phrased with weak distractors. Therefore, this
thesis uses a multi-metric evaluation strategy instead of relying on a single score.

         Traditional text-generation metrics such as BLEU and ROUGE compare a gener-
ated sentence with one or more reference sentences. These metrics are useful when there
is a known target answer, but they are less suitable for open-ended MCQ generation
because many valid questions can be produced from the same lecture paragraph. A low
lexical overlap score does not necessarily mean the generated question is poor, and a
high lexical overlap score does not guarantee that the answer key is correct.

         For this reason, QuizGen evaluates generated questions through task-specific
signals. Retrieval quality is measured using Recall@k and MRR against annotated
source chunks. Source grounding is measured with normalized cosine similarity between

Page 32 of 103 - AI Writing Submission                              Submission ID trn:oid:::1:3572025423
Page 33 of 103 - AI Writing Submission  Submission ID trn:oid:::1:3572025423

generated question content and source material. Bloom alignment is measured with
KL divergence between the target and observed Bloom distributions. Overall quality is
estimated by an LLM judge using a rubric that includes relevance, correctness, clarity,
and groundedness.

         This combination has two advantages for a thesis-scale evaluation. First, it sep-
arates the effects of retrieval, pattern conditioning, and generation quality, making it
possible to explain why one baseline performs better than another. Second, it acknowl-
edges the limits of automation: the metrics support comparison and error analysis, but
they do not remove the need for lecturer review before high-stakes use.

2.8 Research Gap

         The preceding review reveals a consistent pattern across existing systems: re-
search and commercial tools tend to address question generation in isolation, optimising
for fluency or accuracy while neglecting the broader assessment workflow. Specifically,
four gaps remain unaddressed in the existing literature.

         First, most systems generate questions without retrieval grounding. They rely on
the LLM's parametric knowledge rather than the specific lecture materials uploaded by a
lecturer, making the output unverifiable and potentially irrelevant to the course content.

         Second, exam-pattern conditioning is largely absent. No reviewed system allows
a lecturer to supply sample exam questions and use them to condition the style, structure,
and difficulty distribution of the generated output.

         Third, Bloom's taxonomy control is not integrated into the generation pipeline.
While educational theory recommends distributing assessment questions across cognitive
levels, existing systems do not enforce or evaluate this distribution.

         Fourth, no reviewed system integrates question generation with a student practice

Page 34 of 103 - AI Writing Submission                 Submission ID trn:oid:::1:3572025423

workflow. The generated questions are treated as a one-time output rather than as
content for an interactive learning environment with attempt tracking, score history, and
confidence trend visualisation.

         QuizGen addresses all four gaps within a single unified system. It combines
semantic retrieval from uploaded lecture materials, exam-pattern conditioning via few-
shot prompt injection, explicit Bloom-level and difficulty control, a grounding evaluation
component, and a web-based quiz practice interface with per-quiz performance analytics.
This combination distinguishes QuizGen from all systems reviewed in this chapter
and positions it as a more complete solution for lecture-grounded, pattern-aware MCQ
generation and practice.

3.2 Design Requirements and Architectural Decisions

         The architecture of QuizGen was shaped by five design requirements. First,
the system had to be usable as an end-to-end thesis prototype, meaning that all major
functions should be available through a web interface rather than only through scripts.
Second, the system had to keep generated questions traceable to uploaded lecture
materials. Third, the generation pipeline had to support exam-pattern conditioning
without requiring model fine-tuning. Fourth, evaluation outputs had to be reproducible
enough to support thesis claims. Fifth, the implementation had to remain small enough
to run on a student development machine.

         I made several architectural decisions in response to these requirements. For
the backend I chose a modular FastAPI application. It provides clear API boundaries,
automatic OpenAPI documentation, and dependency injection for authentication, while

                                                   24

Page 36 of 103 - AI Writing Submission  Submission ID trn:oid:::1:3572025423

integrating well with Python-based document processing libraries. On the frontend,
Next.js supports a responsive route-based workflow with separate pages for quiz creation,
practice, attempt review, history, dashboards, settings, and batch generation.

         SQLite was selected for persistence because it is sufficient for the single-user
and small-classroom scale of the thesis prototype. The design does not depend on
SQLite-specific features; the main entities can be moved to PostgreSQL in future work.
Similarly, embeddings are stored in the relational database rather than in a separate
vector database to keep the deployment simple. This is acceptable for the thesis dataset,
where the number of chunks is small enough for in-process cosine-similarity search.

         The LLM provider layer was separated from the generation logic so that provider
failures do not require changes to prompts or API endpoints. The llm_router service
receives a normalized request, selects a provider, records usage metadata, and returns a
normalized response. This separation allows the rest of the system to treat Groq, Gemini,
OpenRouter, and Ollama as interchangeable generation backends. It also supports the
usage dashboard by logging latency, status, and token consumption for every provider
call.

3.3 Document Processing and Chunking

         When a user uploads a lecture file, the backend extracts its raw text content and
splits it into overlapping word-level chunks. Three file formats are supported: PDF,
DOCX, and PPTX. Each format is handled by a dedicated parser in the document
processor service, which normalises the extracted text before chunking.

3.4 Embedding and Retrieval

         After chunking, each text segment is converted into a dense vector representation.
The default embedding path uses gemini-embedding-001; if Gemini embedding calls are
unavailable and an OpenRouter key is configured, the router can fall back to the config-

table alongside the chunk text and a reference to the parent document. Because embed-

embedding API calls for the source side.

a 3072-dimensional embedding by default and supports configurable output dimension-

         Cosine similarity is used rather than Euclidean distance because the retrieval task
is primarily about semantic direction in the embedding space. The implementation in
embedder.py explicitly normalizes the query vector and stored chunk vectors before
computing the dot product. This makes the score less sensitive to vector magnitude and
more focused on whether the query and chunk point toward similar meanings. A plain
dot product would mix semantic direction with vector length, while Euclidean distance
would treat absolute position and scale as more important than the angular relationship
that embedding-search systems commonly use for semantic similarity.

         The default retrieval path proceeds in two stages. In the first stage,
cosine_search returns the top-k chunks by cosine similarity; the core evalua-
tion fixed k = 3 for the RAG-only and full-system baselines, while the production default
is k = 8. In the second stage, select_relevant_chunks applies an independent
ceiling of max_chunks=8 on how many of those retrieved chunks are forwarded to the
generator. In the evaluation, k = 3 meant that at most three chunks were ever retrieved,
so the max_chunks ceiling never became the binding constraint; the ceiling matters for
future experiments that raise k beyond 8. Both parameters are configurable through the
service interface and the stored configuration snapshot without changing the database
schema.

         After the advisor review, I extended the evaluation runner with two optional
retrieval modes for ablation analysis. The hybrid mode combines dense cosine retrieval
with BM25 lexical retrieval [17]. Dense retrieval helps when the query and source use
different wording for the same concept, while BM25 helps preserve exact keywords,
abbreviations, and positive/negative qualifiers. The two ranked lists are combined using

3.8 Grounding and Hallucination Proxy

         After generation, each MCQ is scored by the grounding evaluator, which esti-
mates the degree to which the question text, correct answer, and explanation are supported
by the source chunks. The evaluator concatenates these three fields into a single passage
and computes its cosine similarity against the source document embedding. Because
raw cosine similarity lies in [-1, 1], it is normalised to [0, 1] using the transformation
s = (cos + 1)/2 before applying the grounding thresholds.

                                        34

Page 46 of 103 - AI Writing Submission           Submission ID trn:oid:::1:3572025423
Page 47 of 103 - AI Writing Submission                                             Submission ID trn:oid:::1:3572025423

        More formally, for a generated question qi, the evaluator builds:

                                        pi = questioni || answeri || explanationi

where || denotes string concatenation. It then embeds pi and the source document text
into vectors pi and d. The raw semantic score is:

                                        cos(pi, d)   =         pi · d
                                                        pid + 10-12

and the reported grounding score is:

                                        si = max(0, min(1, (cos(pi, d) + 1)/2)).

For example, if a generated DBMS question and its explanation produce a raw cosine
similarity of 0.46 against the uploaded database lecture text, the normalized grounding
score is (0.46 + 1)/2 = 0.73, which falls into the well_grounded range. If a different
question produces a raw cosine similarity of -0.10, the normalized score is 0.45 and the
item is flagged as poorly_grounded. These scores are not proof of factual correctness;
they are triage signals that help reviewers focus on suspicious items.

3.11 Security and Reproducibility Considerations

         Although QuizGen is a thesis prototype, the system includes several security
controls because the workflow handles uploaded documents, generated assessment
content, and student attempt records. User accounts are protected by hashed passwords,
and authenticated API requests use JWT bearer tokens. Protected endpoints resolve the
current user through a backend dependency instead of trusting a user identifier supplied

                                                   38

Page 50 of 103 - AI Writing Submission             Submission ID trn:oid:::1:3572025423
Page 51 of 103 - AI Writing Submission  Submission ID trn:oid:::1:3572025423

by the client. This design prevents a user from reading or modifying another user's
documents, patterns, generations, or quiz attempts through ordinary API calls.

         Uploaded files are processed only after the backend checks their extension and
size. Unsupported formats are rejected, and the maximum upload size is bounded
by configuration. Secrets such as API keys and JWT signing keys are loaded from
environment variables, not hard-coded in source files. These controls are basic, but they
are important for a system that may eventually handle private lecture material.

         Reproducibility is handled at two levels. At the application level, each generation
record stores the prompt version, provider, model, source document, optional pattern,
selected difficulty settings, and generated question payload. At the evaluation level,
the experiment runner stores CSV outputs for each run, aggregated comparison tables,
and failure-analysis reports. The post-review evaluation runner also writes checkpoint
records after completed cases, so a long run can be inspected even if quota limits or
process interruption stop the experiment before all baselines finish. Together, these
records allow the thesis results to be checked against the concrete configuration that
produced them.

         The main reproducibility limitation is the use of external LLM APIs. Even
with fixed prompts and a fixed random seed in the evaluation runner, provider-side
model updates can change outputs over time. For this reason, the thesis reports the
cached evaluation run used for the final comparison and treats future reruns as additional
evidence rather than as replacements for the documented result set. The implementation
also supports provider fallback through OpenRouter auto-free models and a local Ollama
route. These fallbacks improve demonstration resilience under quota pressure, but they
are treated as engineering support rather than as a primary research contribution.

accuracy at self-classification, which is treated as a limitation in Section 6.4. Lower
KL divergence indicates that the generated set follows the requested cognitive mix
more closely. In practice, a high KL value usually means that the model over-produced
lower-order questions such as remember and understand, even when the request asked
for apply or analyze.

every answer option is factually correct. This is why the thesis reports both automated
metrics and qualitative failure analysis.

5.3 Baseline Rationale

         The three baselines isolate the contribution of each major component. The vanilla
baseline represents the simplest practical use of an LLM: sending course content and
asking for MCQs without retrieval or pattern conditioning. It establishes the reference
point for what a generic LLM workflow can achieve.

         The RAG-only baseline adds semantic retrieval but removes exam-pattern con-
ditioning. This baseline tests whether grounding alone improves question quality. If
RAG-only improves semantic grounding but still performs poorly on Bloom KL diver-
gence, then source retrieval and cognitive-level control can be understood as separate
problems.

         The full-system baseline adds both retrieval and pattern conditioning. It therefore
represents the proposed QuizGen pipeline. Comparing the full system against RAG-only
measures the additional effect of pattern and Bloom control, while comparing it against
vanilla measures the combined effect of all proposed components.

         All baselines use the same generation model during evaluation. This avoids a
common confound in LLM experiments: a stronger model can hide weaknesses in the
surrounding pipeline. By holding the model constant, the experiment focuses on whether
the architecture improves the output.

5.5 Practical Interpretation of Results

         I read these quantitative results as evidence for the main thesis claim: question
generation quality depends on more than the language model alone. The largest improve-

                                                   65

ment in semantic grounding occurs when retrieval is added. This is expected because
the model receives a smaller and more relevant context window rather than a broad
document context. In practical terms, this means a lecturer is more likely to receive
questions connected to the uploaded lecture topic instead of questions drawn from the
model's general knowledge.

         The largest improvement in Bloom alignment occurs when pattern conditioning
is added to the RAG pipeline. This indicates that retrieved evidence helps the model
decide what to ask about, while pattern and Bloom instructions help the model decide
how to ask. The two components therefore serve different roles. Retrieval improves
source relevance; pattern conditioning improves assessment-shape alignment.

         The slight reduction in semantic grounding from RAG only (0.9369) to full
system (0.9334) should not be interpreted as a failure of the proposed method. The
difference is small, and the full system gains substantially in Bloom alignment and judge
score. A likely explanation is that pattern conditioning encourages the model to rephrase
source ideas into more exam-like stems and distractors, which can lower raw embedding
similarity while improving pedagogical usefulness.

         The diversity metric also requires careful interpretation. In creative text genera-
tion, higher diversity is usually desirable. In lecture-grounded MCQ generation, however,
too much diversity may indicate that the model is drifting away from the source topic.
The lower diversity of RAG-based methods is therefore acceptable when the objective is
assessment grounded in a narrow lecture context.

5.8 Practical Implications for Lecturers and Students

         For lecturers, the main implication is that LLM-based question generation should
be treated as assisted drafting, not automatic exam authoring. RAG and pattern condi-
tioning improve grounding and alignment, but the failure analysis shows that Bloom
mismatch can still occur. A lecturer should therefore review generated questions, inspect
low-grounding items, and adjust or remove weak questions before using the output in
formal assessment.

         For students, the system is most useful as a formative practice tool. The questions
are generated from lecture material, the quiz interface provides immediate scoring, and
the attempt review page shows explanations after submission. The confidence trend can
help a student see whether repeated practice on the same topic is improving performance.
This closes the loop between generated content and learning feedback.

         For system designers, the results suggest that a useful educational AI application
should combine generation with workflow support. A standalone prompt can create
questions, but it does not manage source files, preserve configuration, track attempts,
evaluate grounding, or show progress over time. In QuizGen, these surrounding features
are not merely interface details; they are part of what makes generated questions usable
in an educational setting.

         The results also mark the boundary of automation. QuizGen can score grounding
and summarize trends, but it cannot fully judge pedagogical appropriateness, cultural
context, course emphasis, or distractor fairness. Those decisions remain part of the

5.9 Threats to Validity

         Several limitations affect the interpretation of the evaluation results.
         Dataset scale. The evaluation covers ten topics across four documents in two lan-
guages. This scale is sufficient for a thesis-level proof of concept but is not large enough
to make broad claims about generalisability across all subject domains or languages.
         LLM judge bias. The judge model (gemini-2.5-flash) assigns scores based on
its own training distribution. It may be systematically lenient or strict across topics and
may correlate with the generation model through shared training data. The strict rubric
and the instruction to use the full scoring range mitigate but do not eliminate this bias.
         Grounding score as a proxy. Semantic grounding measures cosine overlap
between question embeddings and source chunk embeddings. A high score indicates
topical alignment but does not guarantee factual correctness. A well-grounded question
could still contain an incorrect answer option that is semantically close to the source text.
         Retrieval ceiling effect. Recall@k and MRR were 1.0 for all baselines because
the golden dataset is small and each topic has a precisely annotated ground-truth chunk
that the retriever always ranks first. On a larger or noisier document collection, retrieval
metrics would likely show meaningful differences between baselines.
         Single-model evaluation. All three core baselines use the same generation
model. Results may differ with other models; multi-model comparison baselines are
configured in eval/config.yaml but their results were not collected for this thesis and
are left as future work.
         Local deployment only. The system has not been tested under concurrent
load or with a production database. Latency and throughput characteristics may differ

5.10 Summary of Findings

         The evaluation leads to three main findings. First, adding RAG to vanilla LLM
generation substantially improves semantic grounding, from 0.7912 to 0.9369, and LLM
judge quality, from 3.783 to 4.000. This supports the premise that source retrieval is nec-
essary for lecture-grounded MCQ generation. Second, adding exam-pattern conditioning
to the RAG pipeline reduces Bloom KL divergence by 66.5%, from 11.636 to 3.905,
showing that few-shot pattern injection can improve cognitive-level alignment without
model fine-tuning. Third, the full system achieves the best LLM judge score, 4.075,
while maintaining high semantic grounding, 0.9334. The main remaining limitation is
that Bloom distribution control is not perfectly consistent at the individual topic level,
especially for higher-order cognitive levels, so lecturer review before formal exam use
remains necessary.

                  CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK

6.1 Conclusion
         In this thesis I designed, built, and evaluated QuizGen, a pattern-aware retrieval-

augmented generation platform for producing grounded multiple-choice questions from
lecture materials. The work addresses four limitations identified in the reviewed literature:
unsupported LLM generation, weak alignment with lecturer exam patterns, limited
Bloom-level control, and the lack of an integrated quiz practice workflow.

         In the implemented workflow, PDF, DOCX, and PPTX lecture documents are
converted into overlapping word chunks, embedded through the configured embedding
provider, and searched with cosine similarity during generation. Retrieved chunks and
optional exam-pattern examples are then placed into the prompt, allowing the LLM
provider to return structured MCQs with grounding status for each item. Around this
generation pipeline, the web application supports question review, quiz practice, attempt
review, history, usage tracking, and dashboard visualization.

         The evaluation used ten topics in English and Vietnamese with three repeated
runs per baseline. RAG improved semantic grounding from 0.7912 to 0.9369 over vanilla
LLM prompting. Pattern-aware generation reduced Bloom KL divergence by 66.5%
compared with RAG only, from 11.636 to 3.905. The full system also achieved the
highest LLM judge score, 4.075, while returning all requested questions in every run.
Taken together, these results support the claim that retrieval, exam-pattern conditioning,
and Bloom control are useful components for lecture-grounded MCQ generation. They

6.4 Limitations

         Several limitations should be noted. First, QuizGen focuses on MCQ generation;
open-ended, short-answer, and essay questions are outside the current scope. Second,
the evaluation uses a thesis-scale dataset of ten topics across four documents, so the
results may not generalize to all subjects, languages, or document formats. Third,
testing was carried out in a local prototype setting rather than in a classroom deployment
with many concurrent users. Fourth, the grounding score is a semantic proxy: it can
highlight source-support risk but cannot prove factual correctness. The post-review
answer verifier reduces this risk but does not remove the need for lecturer checking.
Fifth, the current generation API is single-document: although the batch workflow
accepts multiple document identifiers, it creates independent quizzes per document
rather than one merged quiz with a controlled 50/50 or proportional split. Sixth, the
evaluation uses LLM-based judges and verifiers rather than a panel of domain-expert
lecturers, so human assessment of relevance, distractor quality, answer correctness, and
pedagogical usefulness remains necessary.

6.5 Future Work

         Future work can extend QuizGen in several directions. True multi-document
retrieval would allow quizzes to be generated from an entire module rather than one
lecture file, with either length-weighted or user-specified proportional sampling and
topic-confusion guard rails. Retrieval can also be strengthened through better keyword
expansion, document-schema-aware signals such as headings or slide numbers, and more
calibrated reranking.

         Generation-side future work includes verifying whether difficulty labels match

                                                   75

Page 87 of 103 - AI Writing Submission  Submission ID trn:oid:::1:3572025423
Page 88 of 103 - AI Writing Submission                 Submission ID trn:oid:::1:3572025423

the actual cognitive demand of each question, extending a main idea across Bloom levels
such as definition-to-apply transformations, and adding a pattern-variation engine that
can safely create same-form, changed-number, fill-in-the-blank, and tricky-distractor
variants. These extensions require strong answer verification because uncontrolled
variation can easily break correctness.

         Evaluation and deployment also need expansion. A larger golden dataset across
more subjects, document types, and languages would strengthen the empirical evaluation,
while human lecturer evaluation would validate relevance, pedagogical value, answer
correctness, and distractor quality. Reinforcement learning from human feedback, as
used in instruction-tuned LLMs [28], is a longer-term direction for learning from lecturer
preferences, but it is outside the scope of this prototype. PDF export, spaced repetition,
a production-grade database, asynchronous generation jobs, concurrency testing, and
formal evaluation of local LLM fallbacks remain practical deployment improvements.

         APPENDIX F: SETUP AND RUN INSTRUCTIONS

         The application can be started locally on Windows with the provided batch script:
start-all.bat

         The script starts the backend on http://localhost:8000 and the frontend on
http://localhost:3000. After startup, the backend health endpoint can be checked
with:
GET http://localhost:8000/api/health

         For containerized execution, the application can be started with Docker Compose:
docker compose up --build

         The recommended demo sequence is:
        1. Log in with a prepared demo account.
        2. Upload or select a lecture document.
        3. Select either no pattern or a saved exam pattern.
        4. Configure question count, language, and difficulty distribution.
        5. Generate a quiz and inspect the review page.
        6. Start the quiz, submit answers, and open the attempt review page.
        7. Open the dashboard to show confidence trend and Bloom breakdown.
        8. Open the usage dashboard to show provider usage and fallback status.
        9. Open the evaluation dashboard to show the cached baseline results.
